Disability as A Human Experience

"Disability" refers to any condition of the body or mind that impairs function, and makes it more difficult for the person to do certain activities and interact with the world around them. When one thinks of disability, the image conjured is often a non-ambulatory person confined to a wheelchair, unable to walk. It is an image that is external to the self, something that happened to another, but cannot happen to the self. However, disability can take many forms, many appearances, and can occur in many stages. It can occur at any point, at any age, can be permanent or temporary, can be visible or invisible. It can be the result of an accident, illness, or a natural part of aging. It can be physical, sensory, cognitive, or emotional. A pregant woman, a person with a broken arm or a sprained ankle, or someone leaving the eye doctor with dilated pupils, are all disabled just as a person with a chronic illness, a person with a mobility impairment, or a person with a sensory impairment is disabled. The only differences are that the former examples are temporary, and the later are permanent, and all have varying levels of impact upon life.

Models of How Disability is Viewed in Society

The Medical Model of Disability places the burden and responsibility on the person with a disability. The person is the problem or owns the problem. The Medical Model of Disability is harsh. It uses keywords such as ‘abnormality’, ‘lack of ability’ and ‘disadvantage’ to describe a person. The Medical Model of Disability applies the medical world’s view that one needs cures or fixes to be a normal functioning person.

Such a harsh view can isolate, stigmatize, and discriminate.
The Social Model of Disability does not place the burden and responsibility on the person with a disability. Instead, this model describes disability as a consequence of environmental, social and attitudinal barriers. Instead of fixing or curing a person with disabilities, it is up to society to remove barriers so as to improve his or her life. Hence, this model provides a deeper understanding and acceptance of people with disabilities.
Both in the physical world, and in the digital world, society must strive to ensure that the world is as accessible as possible to those with impairments that may affect how they interact with said world.

Source for this information: Understand the Social Needs for Accessibility in UX Design by Ruby Zheng

What is Web Accessibility?

Web accessibility means ensuring that websites and applications are usable by everyone, including people with disabilities. This includes people with:

  • Visual impairments: Blindness, low vision, color blindness
  • Hearing impairments: Deafness, hard of hearing
  • Motor impairments: Limited mobility, tremors, lack of fine motor control
  • Cognitive impairments: Learning disabilities, dyslexia, cognitive disabilities

The Law for Accessibility

Americans with Disabilities Act, 1990 prohibits discrimination on the basis of disability in employment, programs and services provided by state and local governments, goods and services provided by private companies, and in commercial facilities

U.S. Department of Justice, 1996 upheld that entities covered by the ADA must also ensure that all avenues of communication are accessible to individuals with disabilities, including websites and apps, thus extending the ADA to include software.

Quick Facts
  • 15% of the world's population has some form of disability
  • Accessibility benefits everyone, not just people with disabilities
  • Accessible websites perform better in search engines
  • Legal compliance is important (ADA, GDPR, etc.)
Why It Matters

Creating accessible software isn't just the right thing to do—it's good business practice. Accessible design benefits:

  • Older adults
  • People on mobile devices
  • People in noisy environments
  • People with temporary injuries

Accessibility benefits everyone.

Semantic HTML

Using semantic HTML elements helps assistive technologies such as screen readers understand the structure and meaning of your content.

✓ Good Example: 
<header>
  <nav>...</nav>
</header>
<main>
  <article>
    <h1>Article Title</h1>
    <p>Content here...</p>
  </article>
</main>
<footer>...</footer>
✗ Bad Example: 
<div class="header">
  <div class="nav">...</div>
</div>
<div class="content">
  <div class="article">
    <div class="title">Article Title</div>
  </div>
</div>

Color Contrast

Sufficient color contrast ensures text is readable for people with low vision and color blindness.

WCAG Standards:

  • Level AA: 4.5:1 contrast ratio for normal text
  • Level AAA: 7:1 contrast ratio for normal text
Good Contrast: Dark text on light background (21:1 ratio)
Bad Contrast: Light text on light background (1.3:1 ratio)

Do Not Use Colors Alone

Color alone for feedback is insufficient for accessibility, as those with colorblindness may not perceive the color difference and may not be able to accurately read the feedback.

✓ Good Example:
✓ Question 10: Sweet Tea
✗ Question 10: Sweet Tea
Correct! Question 10: Sweet Tea
Incorrect. Question 10: Sweet Tea
✗ Bad Example:
Question 10: Sweet Tea

Alt Text for Images

Alternative text describes images for screen reader users and appears when images fail to load.

✓ Good Example: 
<img src="sunset.jpg" alt="A colorful sunset over the ocean with pink and orange clouds">
✗ Bad Example: 
<img src="sunset.jpg" alt="image">
<img src="sunset.jpg" alt="">
<img src="sunset.jpg">

Videos Should Use Captions

Providing captions for videos ensures that users who are deaf or hard of hearing, or who do not have English as their first language, can access and follow along with audio content. Auto-generated captions can be helpful, but they may not always be accurate, so it's important to review and edit them when possible to ensure they are helpful and accurate.

The Myth of the Minority User

The Myth of the Minority User is a common fallacy in tech and design, where product developers mistakenly categorize people with disabilities or specific accessibility needs as an "edge case" or an isolated minority. In reality, individuals with disabilities represent a massive, diverse user base that directly benefits all users when accommodated.

Anywhere between 1 in 5 and 1 in 7 people in the US have a disability. The 2012 Census Bureau study found that in the US there are 57.6m disabled people, 8.1 million of which were visually impaired. For comparrison, that is almost the same numbeber of people as there were residents in New York City in the same year!

The Curb Cut Effect

The Curb Cut Effect refers to the idea that accessibility improvements for people with disabilities often benefit everyone else as well. For example, curb cuts designed for wheelchair users also help parents with strollers, cyclists, individuals with canes or rollators, and the elderly.

WCAG 2.1 Guidelines

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) are the international standards for web accessibility. They are organized around four principles:

1. Perceivable

Information and user interface components must be presentable to users in ways they can perceive. Examples:

  • Provide text alternatives for images
  • Provide captions for videos
  • Ensure sufficient color contrast
  • Make content readable and understandable
2. Operable

User interface components and navigation must be operable. Examples:

  • Make all functionality keyboard accessible
  • Avoid content that causes seizures
  • Provide ways to help users navigate and find content
  • Avoid timed responses
3. Understandable

Information and user interface operation must be understandable. Examples:

  • Make text readable and understandable
  • Make pages appear and operate in predictable ways
  • Help users avoid and correct mistakes
  • Use clear labels and instructions
4. Robust

Content must be robust enough to be interpreted reliably by assistive technologies. Examples:

  • Use valid HTML
  • Use proper ARIA attributes
  • Ensure compatibility with assistive technologies
  • Test with real assistive technologies

Keyboard Navigation

Not everyone can use a mouse. Ensure all functionality is accessible via keyboard.

✓ Good Example: 
<button type="button" 
        onclick="handleClick()">
  Click Me
</button>

<a href="/page" 
   tabindex="0" 
   role="button"
   onkeypress="handleKeyPress(event)">
  Link Button
</a>
Tips:
  • Use semantic HTML buttons and links
  • Ensure focus indicators are visible
  • Manage tab order with tabindex
  • Handle both click and keyboard events

Form Accessibility

Accessible forms help all users complete tasks accurately.

✓ Good Example: 
<label for="email">
  Email Address:
</label>
<input type="email" 
       id="email" 
       name="email"
       aria-describedby="email-help"
       required>
<small id="email-help">
  We'll never share your email
</small>
Best Practices:
  • Always use <label> elements
  • Associate labels with inputs using for attribute
  • Use appropriate input types
  • Provide clear error messages
  • Use ARIA attributes when needed
Conformance Levels

Level A: Basic accessibility features

Level AA: Enhanced accessibility (recommended)

Level AAA: Enhanced accessibility (specialized)

Most organizations aim for WCAG 2.1 Level AA compliance.

ARIA Overview

ARIA (Accessible Rich Internet Applications) provides additional semantics for interactive components.

Accessibility Testing Tools

Use these tools to test and improve your website's accessibility:

Automated Tools
Axe DevTools

Browser extension for automated accessibility testing. Great for catching common issues.

WAVE

Web accessibility evaluation tool. Provides visual feedback about accessibility issues.

Lighthouse

Built into Chrome DevTools. Includes accessibility audits alongside performance metrics.

WebAIM Contrast Checker

Check color contrast ratios to ensure WCAG compliance.

Manual Testing
  • Keyboard Navigation: Test using only Tab, Enter, and arrow keys
  • Screen Reader Testing: Use NVDA (free), JAWS, or built-in screen readers
  • Mobile Testing: Test with mobile accessibility features enabled
  • Color Blindness: Use simulation tools or test with color blind users
